Learning plan

Learn ZBLL one set at a time, and one group of 12 cases at a time within each set. Here's the order most people use, and a routine for each week.

The order

  1. T and U. Common, fairly easy to recognise, with lots of nice algs. Start each one with its 2GLL group: those cases only need R and U turns.
  2. L.
  3. H and Pi. H is the smallest set (40 cases).
  4. Sune and Antisune last. Their OCLL algs are already very fast, so a lot of people leave them until the end, and some skip them. You still gain from learning them.

Why groups of 12? Each group shares one corner pattern, so you learn to tell its 12 edge cases apart together. Learning fewer at once weakens the link between each case and its alg, and learning many more at once gets overwhelming.

A weekly routine

One group of 12 a week works well. An example week:

DaysWhat to do
1–3Learn the 12 algs, 4 a day. For each one: pick an alg, learn it slowly, drill it, and connect it to its case (see Practicing).
4–7Practise the whole group mixed in the trainer, so you have to recognise each case first. Start using them in your solves.
After thatShort reviews, more and more spread out. A few minutes is enough.

At that pace all 472 cases take about 40 weeks. If 12 a week is too much, take it slower: what matters is coming back every day.

ZBTrain's beginner path follows this order for you. It gives you 3 new cases a day (12 in about 4 days), adds the next group once you've started every case in the current one, and schedules your reviews.

Don't learn look-alikes together

Experienced ZB solvers agree on this one: avoid learning very similar groups at the same time. Cases that look alike are easy to mix up while they're new, and untangling them later costs more time than spacing them out now. Get one group comfortable before you start its look-alike.

You also don't have to finish one group completely before starting the next: once you're about a quarter of the way into a group, starting the next one (a different-looking one) keeps things moving.

Choosing algs

  • Look through a couple of sources: ZBLL Explorer collects algs from many sheets, and ZBTrain shows several algs for every case (click a case or its ⓘ).
  • Try 2 or 3 for each case and keep the one that feels best in your hands.
  • Don't overthink it. Two algs that differ by a tenth of a second aren't worth agonising over. If you can't decide, take the first one. You can always switch later.
  • Prefer algs that flow: fewer regrips and rotations usually beat a slightly lower move count.
  • Pick a good alg the first time. Switching algs later is possible, but people who did it often end up with two or three alternatives per case and hesitate between them. A few minutes comparing algs in ZBLL Explorer or a well-known sheet before you learn a group saves that.

Making algs stick

Memorising an alg as a string of letters doesn't last. These habits help you learn algs faster and keep them, even after a break.

Learn in triggers

A trigger is a short move sequence your fingers already know, like R U R' U'. Most algs are a few triggers chained together, so learn them as chunks: "Sune, U2, back Sune" is far easier to remember than 15 separate letters. Triggers also tend to flow without regrips, so they're fast.

TriggerMovesWhere you'll see it
Sexy moveR U R' U'Everywhere: F2L, OLL, and many ZBLLs (often repeated, as in F (R U R' U') F')
Reverse sexyU R U' R'The undo of the sexy move
SledgehammerR' F R F'Pairs and closing moves
HedgeslammerF R' F' RThe undo of the sledgehammer
SuneR U R' U R U2 R'A huge share of 2GLL and Sune/Antisune cases
AntisuneR U2 R' U' R U' R'The undo of the Sune
Back SuneR' U' R U' R' U2 RThe Sune done with R' first, common in H and 2GLL

When you learn a new alg, write it with its triggers marked, for example (R U R' U R U2 R') U2 (R' U' R U' R' U2 R). Many sheets already use brackets like this.

Track the F2L pair

Most ZBLL algs break the F2L for a moment: they take a pair out of its slot, move the last layer around, then pair it up again and put it back. Instead of memorising the moves, follow that pair with your eyes: where it leaves, where it travels, and how it gets rebuilt and inserted.

Once you see an alg as "pair out, do something, pair back in", the moves almost explain themselves, and the alg is much harder to forget. One cuber put it this way: they learned most of 2GLL by tracking the pairs, went on holiday for a month, and could still do most of them from memory when they came back.

Use the Play button in ZBTrain's case window (or step through with ← →) to watch an alg slowly and follow one pair the whole way through.

Watch while you execute

When you're focused on something else, like a timer or the next case, it's easy to run algs on autopilot, and pure muscle memory fades faster than you'd think. Every so often, do your algs slowly and watch what the pieces do. Seeing the alg as well as feeling it gives you a second way to remember it, so a slip in muscle memory doesn't lose the alg completely.

Keep your own sheet

Keep a list of the algs you chose, so you never have to search for them again when you review. A simple way:

  1. Open an existing ZBLL sheet in Google Sheets.
  2. Make your own copy (File → Make a copy).
  3. Replace algs with the ones you chose, and clear the cells for cases you haven't learned yet, so you can see what's left.

Then bring it into ZBTrain on the Sheets page: it reads every tab, checks which case each alg solves, and adds your algs to the trainer. You can also export your algs as a printable PDF.
